Four kinds of holding

Each card carries its own button to show or hide that line on the chart below. These are different jobs in a portfolio, not four options competing for the same dollar.

Class 1 · cash

Short-term government bills

Money you can spend soon, parked in very short US Treasuries. The point is stability and optionality — not growth.

In plain English
You lend to the US government for weeks or months. Price barely moves.
What it is for
Dry powder: surviving a panic without being forced to sell other holdings.
What it is not
It will not keep up with stocks in a boom. Inflation can shrink its real value.
Liquid example on the chart: SGOV
Class 2 · metals

Gold (bullion proxy)

A commodity held as a store of value — not a business with earnings. It does not pay a dividend.

In plain English
A claim on physical gold via a fund. Price moves with real rates, the dollar, and fear.
What it is for
A sleeve that sometimes zigzags when stocks do not. Unreliable on day one of a crash.
What it is not
Not “safe.” A small gold sleeve failed an out-of-sample test — see What does a mix mean?
Liquid example on the chart: GLD
Class 3 · broad index

US large-company basket

Ownership of hundreds of companies at once. This is what most “just own the market” accounts already hold.

In plain English
One share is a tiny slice of the whole US large-cap market.
What it is for
Long-horizon growth of capital. The default measuring stick for other ideas on this desk.
What it is not
Not crisis insurance. Drawdowns of 20–50% have happened and will happen again.
Liquid example on the chart: SPY
Class 4 · one thesis

Geopolitical / commodity idea

A researched story about a shock — here, tanker rates if insurance closes a strait. Concentrated. Can be wrong.

In plain English
Names tied to one mechanism: crude tanker day-rates. Not “the market.”
What it is for
Studying whether a transmission channel shows up in prices. Not a default sleeve.
Fails when
Ceasefire + war-risk premiums unwind; owners refuse to sail so volume dies.
Liquid example on the chart: FRO · one tanker name, not a basket

Same window · indexed to 100, and the fall along the way

Each line starts at 100 on the same day. An ending level on its own is a ranking device, so no line here shows one unaccompanied. Every line also carries its worst peak-to-trough fall, drawn as a dotted drop and stated in the row beneath. The line that ends highest here is also the line that fell furthest, and the two facts belong to the same instrument.

Which of those two numbers matters more depends on whether you would still have been holding at the bottom. That is a question about you, and this page does not answer it.

ILLUSTRATIVE — not a forecast · every line is one example instrument, and one instrument is not its whole asset class

Why these are not competing recommendations

Cash
Answers “can I wait?” — time and nerves.
Metals
Answers “what if money and bonds both struggle?” — a different economic bet.
Index
Answers “what if companies keep earning?” — the market itself.
Thesis
Answers “what if this one mechanism fires?” — research object, not a default mix.
